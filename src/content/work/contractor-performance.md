## The challenge

Contractor performance decisions were slow and fragmented. On average it took 12 weeks to act on a performance concern, and contractors dealt with three different functions outside of their project teams.

> "I feel like we're just recycling bad contractors."
> Design Director

## What was getting in the way

Ownership was spread out. Contractors worked with Talent Acquisition while they were being hired, Resource Management when they were onboarded and cast on projects, and Design Leadership once they were on a project. Each of those touchpoints was brief, so no one had a full picture of someone's actual skills.

Without a shared record, an underperforming contractor could move on to a new project and the next team would rediscover the same issues from scratch.

The only formal input was a PM feedback form that was broken, and no one was tracking whether people used it. PMs were comfortable speaking to delivery, but not to design craft, levelling, or growth. By the time a concern made its way through all three functions, 12 weeks had passed and there weren't many options left.

## The approach

I started manually. Before building any tooling, I ran structured feedback conversations myself. I wanted people to get used to giving feedback, and to trust the process, before any of it was automated.

I also changed who gave feedback. Design Directors and Leads became the main source, since they were best placed to assess design skills and levelling. PMs and other cross-functional leads added what they could speak to well: collaboration, communication, and delivery.

Then I connected the functions, so Talent Acquisition, Resource Management, and Design Leadership were all making decisions from the same information.

## The feedback cadence

At week 2, I did a quick check-in with the Design Directors and Leads working closest to the contractor. Is this person tracking? Anything to watch?

After that, every three weeks we gathered full team feedback on strengths, growth areas, and level, for as long as the project ran.

Every week, a Slack digest went out to Design Leadership, Resource Management, and Talent Acquisition, so everyone stayed updated without needing separate conversations.

## Phase 1: Airtable and a Slack digest

Talent Acquisition's Airtable base held the raw feedback, including who gave it, on which project, how the person was performing against expectations, a levelling assessment, and hiring recommendations. I turned that into a weekly Slack digest timed for the hiring meeting, so Design Leadership could get a quick read on where every contractor stood and which decisions were waiting on them.

## Phase 2: a Notion database for project teams

Phase 1 fixed the decision-making problem for Design Leadership, but another gap showed up. Project teams had no context on a contractor before they started working together. I partnered with our Executive Director of Product Management to make the data useful to Design Directors, Design Leads, and PMs. The Notion database shared themes like strengths, development areas, client-facing ability, and working style, and left out the raw data that wasn't useful to a wider audience.

## How it evolved

Two months in, once the system was running smoothly, I used Claude Cowork to automate the synthesis. The weekly Slack reports and the biweekly Notion updates now generated themselves from Airtable, which kept everything running even in heavy delivery weeks.

The Slack messages asking directors and leads for feedback stayed human. That was the most important signal in the whole system, and it depended on relationships and trust.

## Results

- **2 to 4 weeks** to a performance decision, down from 12
- **4** contractor to full-time conversions in about two months
- **About 20%** negative sentiment in tracked feedback across active contractors, down from about 60%

## Reflection

*Protecting feedback quality.* Design Leadership wanted data earlier. I held the line so teams had enough time working together to give feedback that was actually helpful.

*Coaching for quality.* I spent time up front explaining what useful feedback looked like, especially with cross-functional partners and people earlier in their careers. Metalab had a habit of surface-level "nice" feedback, and I wanted to avoid that.

*Making the case with a prototype.* Design Leadership pushed back on needing a second layer for project teams. Instead of arguing, I gathered what I was hearing from teams and built a lean prototype to show them.

Building trust in the system also took longer than building the system. Directors were used to holding this context informally, and sharing it felt like a real change in how that knowledge was owned.
