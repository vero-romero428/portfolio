## The challenge

Metalab's reputation was built on design quality, but there was no shared definition of what quality meant for the design department.

Leadership couldn't see the quality of in-flight work or how it was trending over time. Quality swung a lot from one project to the next, and when a project struggled, there was no easy way to tell whether the team needed help with process or with skills.

## The opportunity

Work quality was a named department goal, and leadership was accountable for it. The way our leadership put it: our work is our reputation, and our reputation is what keeps us in business.

A framework already existed. One of our Executive Design Directors had written it, and it had been sitting in draft for two years. Like most side-of-desk work at 90% billable utilization, it kept losing out to client deadlines. I was brought in to build the program around it and finally get it in front of the team.

## How we split the work

The Executive Design Director owned the framework content and the craft standards. I owned the program: designing the rollout, facilitating critiques, coordinating the pilot, and working with him and our VP of Design to fit it into rhythms the team already had.

## The plan

I mapped out 19 weeks. The first two were kickoff and pilot setup, then two weeks of critiques and refinements, a pilot from week 6 to 18, and a full department launch in week 19.

## The framework

The framework described what a Metalab product should be, with a short set of questions teams could rate their work against. Some of my favourites: did we follow a strong point of view to make something truly new? Does it meet deeper human needs, and not just transactional ones?

## Gathering feedback

Before anyone had to use it, I ran two rounds of critique on the framework itself. The first was with Design Leadership, and the second was with leaders from engineering and product. I wanted to find the gaps before the pilot did.

We learned a lot. Twelve individual ratings was too much to assess consistently, and the 1 to 7 scale wore people out. The cross-functional round was useful in a different way: engineering and product lenses kept pulling the framework away from design craft, which told us where its boundaries needed to be.

## Pivoting the approach

The feedback also changed who owned quality day to day. Creative Directors became the owners of quality on their own projects, and project critiques moved into a biweekly Creative Director meeting. Design Directors were accountable for quality across all their projects and shared health updates in the biweekly Design Leadership meeting.

## The project quality cadence

Instead of adding a separate review process, I built checkpoints into the project lifecycle the team already followed. In discovery, the team rallies around a north star. In definition, they set a baseline quality rating. From there, they rate and review at each sprint, and visual QA includes a quality and feasibility review.

## The pilot in practice

The pilot showed us things we couldn't see before. The brand team scored consistently high, which helped make the case for growing a small team that was clearly doing great work. Leaner teams had more issues. And projects without a Design Director attached had a visibility gap that nobody had flagged.

## Results

After two quarters, the program moved into maintenance in the DesignOps backlog and became part of company-wide project health scores.

- **3.1 to 4.2:** project health score out of 5, over two quarters
- **[18%]** fewer project quality escalations during resourcing calls

## Reflection

*Get buy-in at every layer, at every checkpoint.* I had exec approval on the framework, but I didn't keep checking in with the layer below as the program moved along. That created tension that could have been avoided.

*Build the baseline before launch.* Looking back, I would have scored a few past projects retroactively with the leadership team. It would have been a low-stakes way to get everyone familiar with the framework and aligned before the pilot started.
