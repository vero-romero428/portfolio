## The starting point

Metalab's growth framework covered about 45 distinct roles. Ten core competencies applied to all ~140 employees. Six disciplines (Design, Engineering, Product Management, User Research and Strategy, Talent Acquisition, and Resource Management) each had their own technical track of 6 to 12 competencies. About 25 roles in Finance, Operations, Sales, IT, and Partnerships relied on the core competencies alone.

## The challenge

A framework designed to support growth had become too heavy to actually serve it.

With hundreds of line items, it captured everything an employee could do but gave little guidance on what strong performance actually looked like. Between cycles it went largely unused. Growth plans were created but rarely revisited, and it became something people only opened when they had to.

## Making the case

I was hearing consistent friction around growth conversations, so I pulled the data to understand the scope and made the case for a full redesign.

- Only 9% of employees had an active growth plan in Lattice.
- Only 33% of managers felt the performance review process was effective.
- Team sentiment around growth and happiness was lower than the previous two years.
- Exit feedback over the prior six months cited stagnation. People felt it was easier to get hired externally at a senior level than to get promoted from within.

## Defining success

Objective: build the conditions that make Metalab a place people grow, so career progression happens from within instead of through attrition and backfill.

- **KR1:** 100% of employees have an active growth plan in Lattice within 90 days of launch.
- **KR2:** Manager confidence increases from 33% to above 60%.
- **KR3:** Reduce attrition tied to growth stagnation, with a promotion pathway that's clear and achievable from within.

## Role and constraints

*Scope.* End-to-end redesign of the core and department competencies, affecting 140 people, with a deeper focus on the 52-person design team.

*Constraints.* It had to work across every department, fit the existing performance cycle, and be simple enough that it wouldn't need a full overhaul every year.

*Key stakeholders.* Design leadership, Engineering leadership, and People Ops, each with different and often mismatched priorities.

## Phase 1: Discovery and audit

I ran 20 sessions with employees, managers, and department leads to find where the framework was creating friction, what was missing, and why people weren't using it. Alongside that, I audited competency overlap, level progression logic, historical promotion feedback, and the language patterns that had piled up over time without adding clarity.

What I found, across the organization:

- The volume created fatigue, and people felt expected to excel at everything.
- Competencies were weighted equally regardless of role or level.
- Early-career employees had no clear signal for what good looked like.
- Competencies were written as passive yes-or-no checkboxes, some tied to things employees couldn't control.
- The framework was rarely used outside review season.

And in the design department specifically:

- Technical competencies no longer reflected how the team worked in a shifting industry.
- The density made self-assessment hard for a visually oriented team.
- A brand designer and a product designer were evaluated against identical criteria.

## Phase 2: Consolidating the core

I reduced nearly 300 line items into observable behavioural signals, rewriting each competency to include 3 to 5 evaluable signals per level.

- Abstract traits became observable actions, so feedback could be specific and backed by evidence.
- Ten core competencies became six by mapping overlap and consolidating: Problem solving and innovation, Communication, Presentation and facilitation, Planning and stakeholder management, Collaboration and teamwork, and Accountability and self-development.
- Clarified level definitions to reduce the judgment calls managers had to make.

*Image: core competency consolidation board*

### Modernizing the design framework

The original framework treated every designer the same. As the team grew and roles got more defined, that stopped reflecting how we actually worked.

- Introduced specialist competencies for Brand and Motion designers.
- Removed outdated responsibilities that were no longer core to the role.
- Formalized critical work that had never been measured, including developer collaboration, specification writing, and cross-functional influence.
- Redefined levels around expanding impact instead of increased output, folding process skills like critique and selling design into broader capabilities.

## Phase 3: Stakeholder alignment

*Engineering* came in with the most detailed feedback. After weighing their pushback on the communication competencies, I updated that content. It made the framework stronger and made them my strongest collaborators.

*Design* wanted more simplification than the core could support. I held that boundary and solved their real concern, usability, by introducing a visual companion instead of reducing the framework's depth.

*People Ops* advocated for roles without technical competencies who relied on the core alone. Keeping it lean meant the framework could serve them instead of overwhelming them.

## Phase 4: Treating the framework like a product

A static document wasn't going to change how people engaged with growth. I partnered with a designer to build a visual companion in Figma that let designers navigate by role and level in a format that felt familiar.

- It made the framework easier to reference day to day.
- It gave managers and employees a shared visual language for honest conversations about progression, achievements, and where to focus next.

*Image: Figma visual companion*

## Rollout

With exec approval secured, my focus shifted to making the framework operational and landing the change.

- Automated the Lattice integration through Zapier, removing manual data entry across every growth path.
- Joined existing design and engineering leadership meetings to walk through the changes in open-format segments.
- Committed to updating the framework ahead of each review season based on incoming feedback.

*Image: Lattice career tracks view*

## Results

The framework moved from a review season artifact to a tool that supported ongoing development.

- **65%** of employees had an active growth plan [X days] after launch, up from 9%.
- **9** promotion packages submitted with framework evidence cited, compared to zero the previous round.
- **68%** of managers felt confident navigating performance reviews after launch, up from 33%, measured by a post-cycle pulse survey.

## Reflection

The biggest challenge was getting a busy organization to stop and engage with it. Two things I'd do differently:

*Build alliances earlier.* The most effective path forward was through managers who genuinely cared. Next time I'd find those people at the start instead of mid-project.

*Design for scale from the start.* The Figma companion was scoped as a design-only pilot. I'd negotiate more time to bring it closer to build-ready and design it to scale across departments from day one.
