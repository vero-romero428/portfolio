## Overview

Improving AI fluency was one of Metalab's 2025 company goals, and our CTO was the executive owner. When the program came to me, the goal was clear but there wasn't much of a plan yet. Over 14 months it grew from 3 sessions a year to 23, with about 40 people at each session. That's roughly 30% of the company each cycle.

## The problem

AI knowledge was all over the place. Some people hadn't found a way to use it in their work yet, while others used it every day. Whatever we built had to work for both.

The feedback from the team was consistent. Projects left no time for learning sessions, and with people in 12 time zones, any single time slot would land in the middle of someone's evening.

Leadership had been going back and forth on which sessions to run, so my first job was to get things moving.

## What I did

### Starting with a pilot

I proposed a pilot of 12 sessions and started looking for speakers. Mostly that meant talking to people around the company and paying attention to their work, to find who was doing something interesting with AI, whether they were power users or just experimenting.

### Working around time zones

I polled the team on days and times. Tuesdays had the least project overlap, so we landed on two Tuesday slots: 10 a.m. Eastern and 3 p.m. Pacific. Together they covered all 12 time zones.

That meant getting creative with facilitation. Sometimes the same session had different facilitators for each slot. For the research and strategy session, two team members led it in the morning and two others led it in the afternoon, each in their own time zone. For sessions that only ran once, we held watch parties with the recording so it still felt social.

### Mixing up the formats

Most sessions were led by our own people from design, research and strategy, engineering, and operations. I also partnered with our Chief Design Officer on fireside chats with industry experts, and we did tool demos, including one with Flora as part of a partnership to bring their tool to the design team.

Some of the most useful sessions were about what AI isn't good at. The research team walked through where tools were falling short for them, and that conversation became the case for the tooling they actually needed.

### Lowering the bar to share

People were more willing to share once it didn't have to be a big presentation. I started Short Shares, 10 minutes on something you did on a project. Some were small workflow shortcuts, and one was a sizzle reel someone made with AI video.

### Using it myself

I used Claude to automate parts of my own ops reporting, turning Airtable entries into weekly Slack digests and biweekly Notion summaries. It meant I could show people a real example from my own work when I talked about it.

## Measuring what worked

After the first six sessions, I sent out a survey. Not many people responded, but the ones who did were very positive. They valued the practical sessions they could apply to their work right away much more than the inspirational, big-picture ones, which helped shape what we booked next. ## Results

- **3 to 23** sessions a year
- **About 40** people per session, roughly **30%** of the company each cycle
- Two session times covering **12 time zones**
- A tooling case for the research team, built from their own feedback

## Reflection

*Mandatory didn't work.* A senior leader's hypothesis was that making sessions mandatory, with CTO backing, would drive attendance. We tried it, and it didn't. At an agency, client work comes first, and that's probably how it should be. Next time I'd spend that energy making the value obvious for each person instead.

*Getting the cadence right took a while.* Industry guests couldn't come twice, and some of the days and times I picked early on didn't suit the teams. Attendance took a hit while I figured that out.
