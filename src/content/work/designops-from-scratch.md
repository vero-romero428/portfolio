## Overview

When I joined Metalab as Design Operations Manager, the design team was 52 people spread around the world, and the craft was excellent. What was missing was the operational and cultural support a distributed team that size needs.

Ops work was split between the Design Directors and the VP of Design, who were each fitting pieces of it around a 90% billable utilization target. At that utilization, there was simply no time for it.

## Where things stood

The team had gone through layoffs about six months before I started, and morale was still recovering. On a distributed team, there are no hallway conversations to help people reconnect, and no one had the time to take that on.

There was also very little shared visibility. Dozens of projects ran at once, each a bit differently, and quality standards lived in people's heads. The data that existed sat in silos and wasn't informing team decisions.

On top of that, DesignOps was a brand new role. People weren't sure what I was there to do, so I knew early on that I'd have to show it through the work.

## Listening first

Before building anything, I did a listening tour. I had one-on-ones with designers at every level and with cross-functional partners, and ran team workshops at the same time. The one-on-ones told me what frustrated people individually. The workshops showed what the team agreed on as a group. Both shaped where I started.

## Creating a path forward

I organized the practice into three streams so the team and leadership could see what DesignOps covered:

- **Business operations and leadership support:** leadership meetings, performance cycles, and working across departments
- **Workflow and tools:** tools, documentation, quality standards, and contractor management
- **People and culture:** the growth framework, onboarding and offboarding, hiring support, and learning

I tracked everything in a DesignOps backlog in Notion and reviewed it with design leadership every quarter. I'd bring what I was hearing from the team, they'd bring what they were seeing from above, and we'd agree on what came next.

The principle I worked from: if it helps the team, it's part of DesignOps.

## The work

### Rebuilding team rhythm

Before any frameworks, the team needed a reason to feel like a team again.

Happy Mondays, a weekly team update, had faded out because no one owned it. I brought it back with a simple format and rotating hosts. Attendance went from about 50% to 78%, and the Slack threads afterward became a good signal of how connected people felt.

GDS, our monthly all-team design session, had become polished and high-pressure, as if every share needed to be your best work ever. I shifted it toward work in progress, including the messy parts. People who had avoided presenting started signing up. Someone in the chat called the Pecha Kucha session "the GOAT session," which I'm still proud of.

### Partnering with design leadership

I took over the weekly Design Leadership meeting. It had a habit of circling without landing anywhere, so I restructured it into working sessions focused on specific problems. Over time it became where team feedback and leadership priorities actually turned into decisions.

### Quality of work

An Executive Design Director had a quality framework stuck in draft. I partnered with him to refine it and build a program around it. Project health went from 3.1 to 4.2 out of 5 over two quarters. [Read the quality of work case study](/work/quality-of-work/).

## Results

- **About 50% to 78%:** Happy Mondays attendance
- **3.1 to 4.2:** project health score out of 5, over two quarters
- For the first time, design leadership had a shared view of ops priorities, reviewed every quarter

The result I'm proudest of is what came next. Engineering leadership saw what dedicated ops support was doing for design and asked for the same. My role grew into Department Operations Manager, covering engineering too, along with company-wide programs like the growth framework redesign and the AI fluency program.

## Reflection

Not having a clear role definition at the start helped more than it hurt. It meant the work got to define the role.

Introducing metrics went better when I started with something people already cared about. Project health felt relevant to everyone's work, so it never felt like measurement for its own sake.

If I did it again, I'd start the quarterly backlog reviews with leadership much sooner. They ended up being one of the most useful parts of the practice.
